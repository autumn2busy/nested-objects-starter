<SectionHeading id="the-1099-reality">The 1099 Reality</SectionHeading>

Here is the uncomfortable truth that hits every new field inspector around April 15th of their first year: nobody withheld taxes from your paychecks.

As a W-2 employee at a previous job, your employer automatically deducted federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare from every paycheck. As a 1099 independent contractor, none of that happened. Every dollar that hit your bank account was gross revenue, and you owe the IRS a percentage of it.

The good news is that 1099 contractors have access to deductions that W-2 employees never see. The mileage alone can slash your tax liability by thousands of dollars. But you have to track everything, and you have to understand what qualifies.

<Callout type="warning" title="The 15.3 Percent Surprise">
Self-employment tax is 15.3 percent of your net earnings. This is on top of your federal income tax rate. If you earned $50,000 net profit as an inspector, you owe approximately $7,650 in self-employment tax alone — before income tax. This is why deductions matter so much.
</Callout>

<SectionHeading id="the-big-deductions">The Major Deductions</SectionHeading>

These are the deductions that move the needle. Every field inspector should be claiming these without exception.

<StepCard number={1} title="Mileage — Your Biggest Weapon">
The IRS standard mileage rate for 2026 is $0.70 per mile. If you drive 25,000 business miles per year (common for full-time inspectors), that is a $17,500 deduction. At 35,000 miles, it is $24,500. This single deduction can reduce your taxable income by more than any other line item. You must choose between the standard mileage rate and actual vehicle expenses — you cannot use both.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={2} title="Vehicle Insurance and Registration">
If you use the actual expense method instead of standard mileage, you can deduct the business-use percentage of your auto insurance premiums, registration fees, and even loan interest. Most inspectors find the standard mileage rate more beneficial, but run both calculations to be sure.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={3} title="Phone and Data Plan">
Your smartphone is your primary business tool. The business-use percentage of your monthly phone bill is deductible. If you use your phone 70 percent for business (inspections, GPS, photo uploads, order management), you deduct 70 percent of the bill. A $100 monthly bill yields $840 in annual deductions.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={4} title="General Liability Insurance">
Your business insurance premiums are 100 percent deductible. If you pay $1,500 per year for general liability and E&O insurance, that is $1,500 off your taxable income.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={5} title="Tools and Equipment">
Every tool you purchase for business use is deductible in the year of purchase under Section 179. Lock sets, drills, winterization kits, measuring wheels, cameras, phone mounts, flashlights, work boots — all of it. Keep every receipt.
</StepCard>

<SectionHeading id="commonly-missed">Commonly Missed Write-Offs</SectionHeading>

These are the deductions that most inspectors either do not know about or forget to claim.

<TipStack>
  <TipCard title="Home Office Deduction">
    If you use a dedicated space in your home exclusively for business administrative work (invoicing, order management, communication), you can claim the simplified home office deduction of $5 per square foot up to 300 square feet ($1,500 maximum).
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Software and App Subscriptions">
    Route optimization apps, mileage trackers, cloud storage for inspection photos, accounting software — all deductible. Even your professional email service.
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Continuing Education and Training">
    Any course, certification, seminar, or training program related to field inspections is deductible. This includes our training tracks, ASHI certification courses, and industry conferences.
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Office Supplies and Printing">
    Clipboards, pens, forms, printer ink, postage for mailing compliance documents — these small expenses add up over a year.
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Health Insurance Premiums">
    If you are self-employed and not eligible for coverage through a spouse's employer plan, you can deduct 100 percent of your health insurance premiums as an adjustment to income. This is an above-the-line deduction, meaning you get it even if you do not itemize.
  </TipCard>
</TipStack>

<SectionHeading id="quarterly-payments">Quarterly Tax Payments</SectionHeading>

The IRS expects you to pay taxes as you earn income, not in one lump sum in April. If you will owe $1,000 or more for the tax year, you are required to make estimated quarterly payments.

| Quarter | Period Covered | Payment Due Date |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | January 1 to March 31 | April 15 |
| Q2 | April 1 to May 31 | June 15 |
| Q3 | June 1 to August 31 | September 15 |
| Q4 | September 1 to December 31 | January 15 (following year) |

<Callout type="tip" title="The 25 Percent Rule">
A simple approach: set aside 25 to 30 percent of every payment you receive into a separate savings account dedicated to taxes. When quarterly payment dates arrive, transfer the accumulated amount to the IRS via IRS Direct Pay or EFTPS. This prevents the April catastrophe.
</Callout>
