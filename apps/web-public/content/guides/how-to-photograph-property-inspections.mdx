<SectionHeading id="why-photos-matter">Why Photo Quality Determines Your Income</SectionHeading>

Your photos are your product. Field services companies do not judge you by how fast you drive or how nice your clothes are. They judge you entirely by the quality and completeness of the photos and data you upload. A blurry, poorly lit inspection report tells the asset manager that you are careless. A crisp, well-organized report tells them you are professional.

The difference between being assigned 5 orders per week and 25 orders per week comes down to one thing: report quality. And the report is 90 percent photos.

Inspectors with high rejection rates get deprioritized in the queue system. Inspectors with zero rejections get priority access to new orders, expanded territory, and higher-paying assignments. The companies track this religiously.

<SectionHeading id="mandatory-shots">The Mandatory Shot List</SectionHeading>

While exact requirements vary by company, here is the universal baseline that covers nearly every field services company's standards.

<InfoStack>
  <InfoBlock title="Address Photo">
    A clear photo of the property's address — either the house numbers on the structure or the mailbox. This is the proof that you visited the correct property. If numbers are missing, photograph the GPS coordinates displayed on your phone.
  </InfoBlock>
  <InfoBlock title="Street View">
    Stand in the street directly in front of the property and photograph the full front of the house with enough context to show neighboring properties. This proves the property exists in a real neighborhood and shows overall curb appeal.
  </InfoBlock>
  <InfoBlock title="Four-Corner Exterior">
    Photograph each of the four sides of the property. Walk to each corner and shoot at an angle that captures two walls in one frame. This gives the reviewer a complete exterior view without you needing to take 20 individual shots.
  </InfoBlock>
  <InfoBlock title="Occupancy Indicators">
    Photograph evidence supporting your occupancy determination. Vehicles in the driveway, curtains in windows, mail accumulation, personal belongings visible — all of these tell a story. Document what you see, not what you assume.
  </InfoBlock>
  <InfoBlock title="Damage and Hazards">
    Capture every visible deficiency. Broken windows, roof damage, foundation cracks, overgrown vegetation, fire damage, vandalism — each gets its own dedicated photo. Wide shot for context, close-up shot for detail.
  </InfoBlock>
</InfoStack>

For interior inspections, add every room individually (including closets and utility areas), all major systems (HVAC unit, water heater, electrical panel), and detailed damage documentation.

<SectionHeading id="technique">Shooting Technique</SectionHeading>

<StepCard number={1} title="Hold Your Phone Steady">
Blurry photos cause instant rejections. Hold your phone with both hands, brace your elbows against your body, and tap the shutter button gently. If you are in a dark interior, lean against a wall or doorframe for stability. Consider a cheap phone grip or tripod mount for your vehicle.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={2} title="Always Use Flash Indoors">
Vacant properties almost never have electricity. Interior rooms without flash produce dark, grainy images that are useless to reviewers. Your phone flash is not great, but it is dramatically better than no flash. For preservation work, consider carrying a small LED flashlight to illuminate rooms from the side.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={3} title="Shoot at Eye Level">
Do not aim your camera upward from waist height. Raise your phone to eye level and shoot straight ahead. This produces a natural perspective that reviewers expect. For damage near the ground (foundation cracks, baseboard water damage), kneel down and shoot at the level of the deficiency.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={4} title="Fill the Frame with the Subject">
If you are photographing a broken window, the broken window should fill most of the frame — not be a tiny detail in the center of a wide-angle shot. Move closer or zoom in slightly. Reviewers should not need to squint to understand what your photo is documenting.
</StepCard>

<SectionHeading id="common-rejects">Reasons Photos Get Rejected</SectionHeading>

<TipStack>
  <TipCard title="Blurry Images">
    Number one rejection reason across the industry. Usually caused by movement during the shutter tap. Hold steady, tap gently, wait a half-second before moving.
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Missing Address Photo">
    You drove to the property, inspected it, uploaded 25 photos, and forgot the one that proves you were at the right house. Always shoot the address first as your opening photo.
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Finger or Thumb Over Lens">
    More common than anyone admits. Check your photos on-screen before driving away. A single obscured photo can trigger a full report rejection.
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Wrong Photo Order">
    Many companies require photos uploaded in a specific sequence. Exterior first, then interior room by room. Uploading out of order forces the reviewer to sort your photos manually, which annoys them and flags your report.
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Dark Interior Shots Without Flash">
    If the reviewer cannot clearly see the condition of a room, your photo is functionally useless. Even a poorly-lit flash photo is infinitely more useful than a pitch-black image with vague shapes.
  </TipCard>
</TipStack>

<SectionHeading id="phone-setup">Phone Setup for Inspectors</SectionHeading>

<RequirementsGrid>
  <RequirementCard>Enable location services and GPS tagging in your camera settings</RequirementCard>
  <RequirementCard>Turn off beauty mode, portrait mode, and AI scene optimization</RequirementCard>
  <RequirementCard>Set photo resolution to the highest available setting</RequirementCard>
  <RequirementCard>Enable HDR mode for balanced exposure in bright and dark areas</RequirementCard>
  <RequirementCard>Clean your camera lens daily — pocket lint causes haze</RequirementCard>
  <RequirementCard>Disable automatic photo backup during inspections to save data</RequirementCard>
</RequirementsGrid>

Your camera is your moneymaker. Treat it accordingly. The inspectors earning the most volume are not the fastest drivers — they are the ones whose photo sets never get sent back for rework.
