<SectionHeading id="situational-awareness">Situational Awareness</SectionHeading>

As a field inspector, you are often visiting people at the lowest point in their lives. They may be facing foreclosure, job loss, or personal crisis. While 99% of interactions are routine, the 1% of hostile encounters requires a professional, prepared response.

Safety starts before you even get out of your vehicle.

<TipStack>
  <TipCard title="The Drive-By Assessment">
    As you pull up, scan for signs of a 'defensive' property: perimeter fencing, large unrestrained dogs, security cameras, or window signage indicated hostility toward outsiders.
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Parking for Escape">
    Never pull into a narrow driveway where you can be 'boxed in' by another vehicle. Park on the street, facing the exit. Leave yourself a clear path to pull away instantly.
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Keep the Engine Running?">
    Some high-risk inspectors leave the engine running or the keys in their pocket (proximity start). Never leave your keys inside a locked, running vehicle at a hostile property.
  </TipCard>
</TipStack>

<SectionHeading id="de-escalation">De-escalation Techniques</SectionHeading>

If you are confronted by an occupant, your goal is to lower the temperature and leave gracefully.

<InfoStack>
  <InfoBlock title="The 3-Second Rule">
    When asked 'What are you doing here?', wait three seconds before speaking. This prevents a defensive, rapid response and shows you are calm.
  </InfoBlock>
  <InfoBlock title="The 'External Authority' Pivot">
    Do not take it personally. Say: 'I am just here to verify the exterior condition for the insurance company/mortgage servicer. I don't have any info on the account balance.' Position yourself as a neutral data-collector.
  </InfoBlock>
  <InfoBlock title="Acknowledge and Retreat">
    If they are yelling, don't yell back. Say: 'I understand you're frustrated. I'll head out now and let my office know you're not comfortable with the visit.' Then walk away—never turn your back completely.
  </InfoBlock>
</InfoStack>

<SectionHeading id="legal-limits">Understanding Your Legal Limits</SectionHeading>

| Action | Legally Permitted? | Professional Advice |
|---|---|---|
| **Walking to the front door** | Usually | Allowed for 'Knock and Notice' unless 'No Trespassing' is posted. |
| **Taking photos from the street** | Yes | Public property photography is protected. |
| **Entering a backyard** | Gray Area | Avoid unless the property is clearly and obviously abandoned. |
| **Entering a dwelling** | No | NEVER enter an occupied dwelling without explicit consent. |

<Callout type="warning" title="No Trespassing Signs">
In many states, a 'No Trespassing' sign carries legal weight. If you ignore it and an occupant calls the police, you may be cited. Note the sign, take a photo of it from the street, and mark the order as 'Access Restricted - Hostile Environment'. You will still get partial or full pay.
</Callout>

<SectionHeading id="technology-safety">Safety Tech for the Field</SectionHeading>

<StepCard number={1} title="Dash Cams">
A front and rear dash cam acts as an unbiased witness to any driveway or street-level altercations.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={2} title="Safety-Check Features">
Use apps like Life360 or the built-in 'Emergency SOS' on your phone. Many field apps also have a 'Check-In' feature designed for safety monitoring.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={3} title="Professional Identification">
Always wear high-visibility gear or a lanyard with your company badge. Looking like a professional contractor reduces the 'suspicious person' trigger in most neighborhoods.
</StepCard>

Your career in field services is a marathon, not a sprint. No single order is worth a physical injury. Learn to trust your gut—if a property feels wrong, move on.
