<SectionHeading id="why-winterization">Why Winterization Pays Premium</SectionHeading>

Of all the work order types available to property preservation contractors, winterization consistently ranks among the highest-paying recurring orders. While a standard occupancy check pays $8 to $15, a winterization pays $50 to $150 — for roughly 45 minutes to 2 hours of work.

The reason is simple: the consequences of getting it wrong are catastrophic. A single burst pipe in a vacant foreclosure can cause $10,000 to $50,000+ in water damage, mold remediation, and structural repairs. The bank that owns the property is on the hook for those costs. They will gladly pay $100 to prevent a $30,000 loss.

This is also the highest seasonal-demand work type in the industry. From October through March, companies are scrambling to find contractors who can actually perform quality winterizations. If you can do this work reliably, you will never lack for orders during winter months.

<Callout type="warning" title="This Is Not Entry-Level Work">
Do not accept winterization orders until you have been properly trained or have worked alongside an experienced contractor. A botched winterization does not just cost you the account — it costs the bank five figures and potentially costs you a liability claim.
</Callout>

<SectionHeading id="the-process">The Step-by-Step Process</SectionHeading>

Every company has slightly different photo and reporting requirements, but the physical winterization process is standardized across the industry.

<StepCard number={1} title="Locate and Shut Off the Main Water Supply">
Find the main water shut-off valve. In most residential properties, this is in the basement near where the water line enters the house, near the water meter, or in a utility closet. Turn it completely off. If the property has a well system, turn off the well pump at the breaker.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={2} title="Drain the Water Heater">
Attach a garden hose to the drain valve at the bottom of the water heater. Run the hose to the nearest floor drain or exterior door. Open the drain valve and the pressure relief valve on top. Let it drain completely. This can take 15 to 30 minutes for a standard 40-gallon tank.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={3} title="Open All Faucets and Flush All Toilets">
Go through every room. Open every hot and cold faucet. Flush every toilet. Run every shower and bathtub faucet. The goal is to drain as much standing water from the supply lines as possible using gravity.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={4} title="Blow Out the Lines with Compressed Air">
Using a portable air compressor and a blow-out adapter attached to an exterior hose bib, force compressed air through the supply lines at 40 to 60 PSI. Work from the lowest point in the system upward. You will hear sputtering from open faucets as the remaining water is pushed out.
</StepCard>

<StepCard number={5} title="Cap or Plug Any Open Lines">
After draining, cap or plug any disconnected or exposed pipes. Use push-fit caps or traditional threaded caps with teflon tape. Document each capped line with a photo for your report.
</StepCard>

<SectionHeading id="antifreeze">Antifreeze Application</SectionHeading>

After the lines are drained and blown out, every drain trap in the property needs antifreeze. Drain traps hold a small amount of water to prevent sewer gas from entering the house. If that water freezes and expands, it cracks the trap.

<InfoStack>
  <InfoBlock title="Toilets">
    After flushing, use a wet/dry vacuum to remove remaining water from the bowl and tank. Pour 1 to 2 cups of RV antifreeze into the bowl. The antifreeze sits in the trap and prevents freezing.
  </InfoBlock>
  <InfoBlock title="Sinks and Tubs">
    Pour 1 cup of RV antifreeze into each sink and tub drain. This fills the P-trap with antifreeze instead of water.
  </InfoBlock>
  <InfoBlock title="Floor Drains">
    Basement floor drains are the most commonly missed traps. Pour 1 to 2 cups of antifreeze into every floor drain. Missing one can cause it to freeze, crack, and flood the basement.
  </InfoBlock>
  <InfoBlock title="Washing Machine and Dishwasher">
    Pour antifreeze into the drain hose connection points. Run the dishwasher for 10 seconds (if power is on) to pull antifreeze into its internal lines. If power is off, pour directly into the sump area.
  </InfoBlock>
</InfoStack>

<Callout type="info" title="Use Only RV Antifreeze">
Never use automotive antifreeze (ethylene glycol) in occupied or potentially reoccupied properties. It is toxic. RV antifreeze (propylene glycol) is non-toxic and specifically designed for potable water systems. It is typically bright pink and costs $3 to $5 per gallon.
</Callout>

<SectionHeading id="common-mistakes">Mistakes That Get You Fired</SectionHeading>

<TipStack>
  <TipCard title="Skipping the Water Heater">
    The water heater holds 40 to 80 gallons of standing water. If you forget to drain it and temperatures drop below freezing, the tank or its fittings will crack. This is the single most expensive winterization failure.
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Missing Hidden Bathrooms">
    Large houses often have half-baths tucked under stairwells or in finished basements that you might not notice during a quick walkthrough. Systematically check every room.
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Forgetting Exterior Hose Bibs">
    Exterior spigots are directly exposed to freezing temperatures. Disconnect any hoses, open the valve, and ensure the interior shut-off for the outdoor line is closed. Some properties have frost-proof hose bibs that still need to be drained.
  </TipCard>
  <TipCard title="Insufficient Photo Documentation">
    If you winterized it but did not photograph it, you did not winterize it. Companies require photos of every step — main shut-off, water heater drain, every fixture with antifreeze, every capped line. Take more photos than required.
  </TipCard>
</TipStack>

<SectionHeading id="seasonal-strategy">Seasonal Revenue Strategy</SectionHeading>

Smart contractors treat winterization season (October through March) as their revenue peak. Here is how to maximize it.

Build relationships with 3 to 5 companies that offer preservation work before September. When October hits and temperatures start dropping, these companies blast out hundreds or thousands of winterization orders. The contractors already on their panel get first pick. If you are still going through compliance screening in November, you have missed the wave.

Stack winterization orders with standard inspection work. Complete drive-by orders in the morning when it is coldest and properties are hardest to winterize, then hit winterization orders in the afternoon when interior work is more comfortable. A blended day of 8 drive-bys ($96) plus 3 winterizations ($300) puts you at $396 for the day.
